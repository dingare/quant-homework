### Verified minimal repair of the user's rewrite

The boolean is renamed for clarity; the three functional fixes above are applied. This is the assistant-verified repair, consistent with the user's later report of completion.

```python
def evaluate(expression: str) -> int:
    stack = []
    total = 0
    sign = 1
    expecting_operand = True
    i = 0

    while i < len(expression):
        s = expression[i]
        if s.isspace():
            i += 1
            continue

        if s.isdigit():
            num = 0
            while i < len(expression) and expression[i].isdigit():
                num = num * 10 + int(expression[i])
                i += 1
            total += sign * num
            sign = 1
            expecting_operand = False
            continue

        if s in '+-':
            if expecting_operand:
                sign *= 1 if s == '+' else -1
            else:
                sign = 1 if s == '+' else -1
                expecting_operand = True

        if s == '(':
            stack.append((total, sign))
            total = 0
            sign = 1
            expecting_operand = True

        if s == ')':
            outer_total, outer_sign = stack.pop()
            total = outer_total + outer_sign * total
            expecting_operand = False

        i += 1

    return total
```

Invariant: total is the sum of completed terms at the current level; when an operand is expected, sign is the effective sign for that next operand. Stack frames save unfinished outer totals and the sign applying to the inner expression. A closing bracket folds one completed inner result into its parent. Each character is scanned once: O(n) time, O(d) extra space for maximum nesting depth d, under the stated arithmetic-cost assumption. Inputs are assumed valid.

```math
P(L=9)=\frac{(1/2)9}{(1/2)1+(1/2)9}=0.9.
```


### Product CDF and density

For 0 less than y less than 1, the joint density is 1 in the unit square:

```math
F_Z(y)=\int_0^y1\,du+\int_y^1\frac yu\,du=y-y\ln y.
```

F=0 for y at most 0 and F=1 for y at least 1. Density is -ln y on (0,1) and zero outside that interval; endpoint values do not affect the distribution.

### Conditional mean and the meaning of the numerator

Let A be the event UV greater than 1/2. Then

```math
P(A)=\frac{1-\ln2}{2},
\qquad E[U\mid A]=\frac{E[U\mathbf{1}_A]}{P(A)}.
```

Integrating 1 over the event gives its probability; integrating u gives the weighted numerator. Either order works:

```math
E[U\mathbf{1}_A]
=\int_{1/2}^1\int_{1/(2u)}^1u\,dv\,du
=\int_{1/2}^1(u-1/2)\,du=\frac18.
```

Or integrate u first:

```math
E[U\mathbf{1}_A]
=\int_{1/2}^1\int_{1/(2v)}^1u\,du\,dv
=\int_{1/2}^1\left(\frac12-\frac{1}{8v^2}\right)dv=\frac18.
```

Thus

```math
E[U\mid A]=E[V\mid A]=\frac{1}{4(1-\ln2)}\approx0.814723.
```

### Conditional dependence and covariance

For a fixed u in (1/2,1), V conditioned on U=u and A is Uniform(1/(2u),1). Its support changes with u, proving dependence. Equivalently, under A, each marginal has positive probability of falling below 0.6, but the joint event U less than 0.6 and V less than 0.6 is impossible.

```math
E[UV\mathbf{1}_A]
=\int_{1/2}^1\int_{1/(2u)}^1uv\,dv\,du
=\int_{1/2}^1\left(\frac u2-\frac{1}{8u}\right)du
=\frac3{16}-\frac{\ln2}{8}.
```

After normalizing,

```math
E[UV\mid A]=\frac{3-2\ln2}{8(1-\ln2)}.
```

```math
Cov(U,V\mid A)
=\frac{3-2\ln2}{8(1-\ln2)}-\frac{1}{16(1-\ln2)^2}
\approx-0.006411884.
```

Nonzero covariance proves dependence. Zero covariance generally does not prove independence. Independent variables before conditioning may become dependent after conditioning on an event involving both.

### Worked reference — supplied during guided review

Let mu_X=E[X_i], mu_Y=E[Y_i] and r=mu_X/mu_Y. Exactly,

```math
\hat r-r=\frac{\bar X-r\bar Y}{\bar Y},
\qquad W_i=X_i-rY_i,\qquad E[W_i]=0.
```

The iid CLT and law of large numbers, followed by Slutsky's theorem, give

```math
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{n}\,\bar W &\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\sigma_W^2),\\
\bar Y &\xrightarrow{p}\mu_Y,\\
\sqrt{n}(\hat r-r)&\xrightarrow{d}N\left(0,\frac{\sigma_W^2}{\mu_Y^2}\right).
\end{aligned}
```

Here the finite second moments ensure a finite variance, and sigma_W squared strictly positive is the nondegeneracy condition. Pairwise covariance must be retained:

```math
\sigma_W^2=\mathrm{Var}(X_i-rY_i)
=\mathrm{Var}(X_i)+r^2\mathrm{Var}(Y_i)-2r\,\mathrm{Cov}(X_i,Y_i).
```

For inference, define residuals using the estimated ratio. Their sample mean is exactly zero:

```math
\hat W_i=X_i-\hat rY_i,\qquad \frac1n\sum_i\hat W_i=0,
\qquad s_W^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i(X_i-\hat rY_i)^2.
```

The limiting variance above is for sqrt(n) times the estimation error. For the unscaled estimator, divide that variance by n, then take a square root and plug in consistent estimates:

```math
\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat r)=\frac{s_W}{\sqrt{n}\,\bar Y},
\qquad \hat r\ \pm\ z_{0.975}\frac{s_W}{\sqrt{n}\,\bar Y},
\qquad z_{0.975}\approx1.96.
```

This is an asymptotic Wald interval, not an exact finite-sample normal or t interval. Positive Y_i makes the sample denominator positive. For fixed positive mu_Y the asymptotic result remains valid; finite-sample accuracy can be poor when denominator uncertainty is large relative to its mean. A small denominator can make the ratio strongly asymmetric, so a local linear approximation and symmetric Wald interval may be unreliable even for a fairly large n. This concern is about relative uncertainty, not small measurement units alone.

If E[|X_i/Y_i|] is finite, the individual-ratio average instead converges to E[X_i/Y_i]. Under independence it factors as E[X_i]E[1/Y_i] whenever these expectations are well-defined and finite. The original moment assumptions do not guarantee integrability of X_i/Y_i.

For X identically 1 and Y Uniform(1,3), both targets exist but differ:

```math
E[X/Y]=E[1/Y]=\frac12\int_1^3\frac1y\,dy=\frac{\ln3}{2},
\qquad \frac{E[X]}{E[Y]}=\frac12.
```

### Worked reference — main probability question only

The stated model output is the exact training posterior. Let pi denote class prevalence. With class-conditional densities f_1(x) and f_0(x), Bayes gives

```math
\begin{aligned}
P(Y=1\mid x)&=\frac{f_1(x)\pi}{f_1(x)\pi+f_0(x)(1-\pi)},\\
P(Y=0\mid x)&=\frac{f_0(x)(1-\pi)}{f_1(x)\pi+f_0(x)(1-\pi)}.
\end{aligned}
```

Dividing cancels the common marginal density and produces

```math
\frac{P(Y=1\mid x)}{P(Y=0\mid x)}
=\frac{f_1(x)}{f_0(x)}\frac{\pi}{1-\pi}.
```

Posterior odds equal likelihood ratio times prior odds: the feature evidence multiplies the starting positive-to-negative odds. Because f_1 and f_0 are unchanged, only prior odds change. For probabilities strictly between zero and one,

```math
\begin{aligned}
\frac{p_{\mathrm{deploy}}(x)}{1-p_{\mathrm{deploy}}(x)}
&=\frac{0.1/0.9}{0.5/0.5}\frac{p_{\mathrm{train}}(x)}{1-p_{\mathrm{train}}(x)}\\
&=\frac19\frac{p_{\mathrm{train}}(x)}{1-p_{\mathrm{train}}(x)}.
\end{aligned}
```

Convert adjusted odds back to probability:

```math
p_{\mathrm{deploy}}(x)=\frac{p_{\mathrm{train}}(x)}{9-8p_{\mathrm{train}}(x)}.
```

This expression also extends to posterior endpoints by continuity. At training output 0.8, training odds are 4:1 and deployment odds are 4:9:

```math
p_{\mathrm{deploy}}(x)=\frac4{13}\approx0.307692.
```

The multiplier 1/9 applies to odds, not directly to probability. Equal priors imply equal posteriors here only together with unchanged class-conditional distributions.
