## Core Question

Why does convex duality sit underneath no-arbitrage pricing?

More concretely: why do portfolio construction problems and state-price valuation problems produce the same no-arbitrage bounds?

## Why This Topic Matters

Convex duality is a mathematical foundation of:

- asset pricing,
- optimization,
- machine learning,
- quantitative finance.

For finance, it explains why:

- replication problems,
- super-hedging problems,
- no-arbitrage price bounds,
- state-price representations

are all different views of the same structure.

QR interviews like this topic because it reveals whether I understand finance geometrically, not just procedurally.

## My Current Understanding

If a payoff can be replicated exactly, no-arbitrage forces a unique price.

If the market is incomplete, exact replication may fail. Then the target payoff is not pinned down by one unique number.

Instead we get price intervals.

Super replication gives the cheapest portfolio that always dominates the payoff.

Sub replication gives the most expensive portfolio that always stays below the payoff.

That is the practical buy-side intuition: in real markets, fair value is often a band rather than a point.

## Primal View

The primal view starts from constructing portfolios.

Let:

- $X$ be the payoff matrix across states,
- $w$ be portfolio weights,
- $p$ be current asset prices,
- $z$ be the target payoff.

Replication asks for:

```math
Xw=z
```

with cost:

```math
p^\top w
```

Super hedge:

```math
\min_w p^\top w
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
Xw\ge z
```

Sub hedge:

```math
\max_w p^\top w
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
Xw\le z
```

Interpretation:

- primal = search over feasible portfolios,
- super hedge = cheapest dominating hedge,
- sub hedge = richest dominated hedge.

## Dual View

The dual view starts from pricing functionals rather than portfolios.

Introduce state prices $q$ such that:

```math
X^\top q=p
```

This means the pricing rule is consistent with observed asset prices.

Then any payoff $z$ is valued by:

```math
q^\top z
```

State prices are not probabilities.

They are valuation weights:

- probabilities describe likelihood,
- state prices describe present value per unit payoff in each state.

Only after normalization and discounting adjustments do they resemble risk-neutral probabilities.

## Why This Initially Felt Confusing

Why does an upper bound come from a minimization:

- because the relevant upper bound is the cheapest portfolio that still dominates the payoff

Why does a lower bound come from a maximization:

- because the relevant lower bound is the most valuable portfolio that still stays below the payoff

Why do two different problems have the same answer:

- because one searches over hedges and the other searches over pricing rules, but both describe the same no-arbitrage boundary

Why are state prices not probabilities:

- because they encode value across states, not likelihood across states

## Strong LP Duality

Primal super-hedging problem:

```math
\min_w p^\top w
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
Xw\ge z
```

Dual problem:

```math
\sup_q q^\top z
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
X^\top q=p
```

Why are they equal in words?

Because the primal asks for the cheapest valid hedge, while the dual asks for the richest valid no-arbitrage valuation. If no-arbitrage is internally consistent, those two frontiers must meet at the same value.

## Geometry

The attainable payoff space is the set of payoffs generated by $Xw$.

This is the core geometric object.

Super-hedging means searching for attainable payoffs that lie above $z$ state by state.

The dual side introduces a linear functional that supports or separates this set.

Key geometric ideas:

- attainable payoff space,
- convex sets,
- supporting hyperplanes,
- separating hyperplanes,
- column spaces,
- linear functionals.

This is why duality feels natural once the geometry is clear.

## Connections to Linear Algebra

This topic connects naturally to:

- column spaces,
- null spaces,
- orthogonal complements,
- linear maps,
- projection.

$X$ maps portfolio space into payoff space.

The column space of $X$ tells us which payoffs are attainable.

The constraint:

```math
X^\top q=p
```

tells us the pricing functional matches market prices on that attainable space.

This is exactly the kind of matrix intuition interviewers want to see.

## Market / Rates Application

This matters in rates because markets are usually incomplete and constrained.

Applications:

- Treasury basis,
- CTD,
- repo financing,
- balance sheet constraints,
- funding costs,
- delivery option,
- no-arbitrage bands.

Why real markets often give intervals instead of one exact fair value:

- financing is costly,
- shorting may be constrained,
- delivery optionality matters,
- balance sheet has shadow cost,
- execution frictions prevent exact replication.

So buy-side rates work often lives in bands, not textbook equalities.
